Step 1: What is sepsis?

Sepsis is the body’s extreme response to an infection. In older adults, it often starts subtly and can progress quickly.

Key idea: Sepsis is not just “a bad infection”. It’s when the body’s response to infection starts damaging its own tissues and organs.

Common sources of infection in aged care

  • Urinary tract infections
  • Chest infections (e.g. pneumonia)
  • Skin and soft tissue infections
  • Abdominal infections

Step 2: Recognising deterioration

In aged care, small changes in vital signs and behaviour can be early signs of sepsis. Use the monitor below to see what “normal” and “concerning” look like.

Respiratory rate
18 breaths/min
Typical: 12–20 ≥22 is concerning
Heart rate
88 bpm
Typical: 60–100 <50 or >110 is concerning
Temperature
36.8 °C
Typical: 36.0–37.5 <36.0 or >38.0 is concerning
Systolic BP
128 mmHg
Typical: 110–140 <100 is concerning
Tip: In older adults, respiratory rate and mental state changes are often more important than temperature alone.

Step 3: Behavioural change as an early sign

Older adults may not show classic signs like high fever. Instead, you might see new confusion, reduced intake, or increased sleepiness.

Red flags worth reporting

  • New or worsening confusion or agitation
  • Not eating or drinking as usual
  • More sleepy or harder to rouse
  • Subtle but clear change from their normal baseline

A resident seems quieter than usual, skipped breakfast, and is more confused this morning.

Step 4: Communicating concern – ISBAR

When you escalate, a clear structure helps the clinician understand the situation quickly. ISBAR is a simple framework.

ISBAR quick guide

I – Identify
Resident name, DOB, location
S – Situation
What’s happening now? Why are you concerned?
B – Background
Relevant history, baseline function, known conditions
A – Assessment
Vitals, observations, specific changes you’ve noticed
R – Request
What you need (review, orders, advice, transfer)

You’re calling the GP about a resident with new confusion and fast breathing.

Step 5: DNR orders and escalation

A “not for CPR” order does not mean “do not treat”. Residents with DNR orders can still be for active treatment, including antibiotics and hospital transfer if appropriate.

Key point: DNR relates only to CPR if the heart or breathing stops. It does not automatically limit other treatments.
