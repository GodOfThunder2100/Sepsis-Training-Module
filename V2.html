What is Sepsis?

Clinical Definition (Sepsis-3 Consensus)

Sepsis is defined as a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. In frail aged-care residents, acute deterioration in organ function is quantified by a rapid change in baseline parameters (such as qSOFA or NEWS2 triggers).

Septic Shock occurs when cellular/metabolic abnormalities deepen, characterized by persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥ 65 mmHg and serum lactate > 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

Simple Definition

Sepsis is a medical emergency. It happens when the body's immune system overreacts to an infection (like a chest or urinary infection) and starts harming its own organs and tissues instead of just fighting the germs.

Without quick treatment, sepsis can turn into septic shock, causing organs to fail fast. In older adults, it can happen in a matter of hours.

>65%of sepsis cases in aged care start from a urinary or respiratory infection
Hoursnot days — how quickly frail older residents can deteriorate

Visual Progression & Presentation

Sepsis causes systemic tissue hypoperfusion. Patients often look acutely toxic, lethargic, or profoundly mottled. Recognising this appearance early — alongside the behavioural changes covered in the next module — is what gives a resident the best chance of a good outcome.

ACSQHC Standard: Always ask "Could it be Sepsis?"
Severe Sepsis: Peripheral cyanosis and skin mottling

Signs & symptoms

Sepsis in older adults rarely looks textbook. Fever is often absent — watch for change from baseline instead. The list on the right is the one aged care staff miss most often, because it looks like "just getting older" rather than a medical emergency.

General signs

  • Fast breathing or shortness of breath
  • Fast heart rate
  • Fever or feeling abnormally cold
  • Clammy, mottled or discoloured skin
  • Extreme pain or physical discomfort

In older adults specifically

  • New or worsening confusion — often the first sign
  • Sudden functional decline — uncharacteristic weakness
  • Reduced appetite or fluid intake
  • Increased falls or unsteadiness
  • "Just not themselves" — a critical red flag

Wester et al., 2013; Clifford et al., 2016

Clinical Note: Diagnostic error rates exceed 10% in older patients due to diagnostic overshadowing from existing comorbidities. Anchor your assessment to the resident's own baseline, not a population norm.
Support Note: You don't need to diagnose anything. Notice the change and report it immediately — the next module gives you the exact words to use.

How to inform the nurse

You are the front-line warning system. Staffing ratios mean your observations save lives — you often spend more one-on-one time with residents than anyone else on shift, which makes your read on "different to usual" genuinely clinical information.

When alerting the nurse, state:

  1. Who — resident name & room
  2. What — exact change observed
  3. How different — compared to baseline
  4. Context — recent falls, missed meals

What this sounds like

Vague — hard to act on

"I think something might be a bit off with Mr Nguyen, not sure though."

Specific — easy to act on

"Mr Nguyen, room 12 — he's refused breakfast and hasn't said a word to me, and he's usually chatty. That started about an hour ago."

Quick reference: ISBAR

Identify, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation — keep this order every time you call for help.

Quick reference: red flags

New confusion, sudden functional decline, reduced intake, "just not themselves" — any one is worth reporting.

Who to contact

Follow your facility's escalation pathway: nurse in charge, then GP or after-hours medical service, then 000 if the resident is critically unwell.
